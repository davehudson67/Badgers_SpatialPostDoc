# 02 — Current results in plain English

## What did we learn about movement itself?

The movement model supports a **dominant local/stable state** plus a **rarer high-mobility/relocation state**.

The high-mobility state corresponds to movement on the scale of several hundred metres to about a kilometre.

The tiny fitted local-state movement scale should not be interpreted literally. It is mainly the Gaussian model's way of representing the large number of years in which animals are observed at the same or very nearby sett.

Males are substantially more likely than females to **enter** a high-mobility state. There is much less evidence that males travel farther than females once they are already in the same movement state.

High mobility is mainly **episodic**, rather than evidence for two permanent kinds of badger.

---

## Does infection make badgers move more later?

The early same-year analysis gave a weak positive tendency.

However:

- adjustment for historical period and entry-age class weakened it;
- requiring infection to clearly precede movement removed the signal;
- within-badger strict-lag analysis was also essentially null.

**Current interpretation:** there is no robust evidence that established infection causes or reliably predicts later high-mobility initiation. There may be same-year temporal coupling, but ordering is unclear.

---

## Does high mobility make badgers more likely to become infected?

The primary population-level model found **no evidence that high mobility generally increases infection acquisition in the following year**.

The posterior leaned below an odds ratio of 1, but uncertainty was broad. Do not call this a protective effect.

The importance-sampling robustness rerun gave the same biological result.

A within-badger case-crossover sensitivity produced positive point estimates, but it was based on very few informative movement-switching event strata and had very wide uncertainty.

**Current interpretation:** no convincing general population-level effect; a within-animal signal remains possible but is weakly identified.

---

## Why are we now looking at local infection pressure?

A high-mobility event may only increase infection risk if the animal moves through or into an area with more infection.

That leads to the current mechanistic question:

> Is high mobility risky only when local/social-group infection pressure is high?

The first analysis uses same-social-group infection prevalence because it is directly interpretable and retains the largest number of infection events.
